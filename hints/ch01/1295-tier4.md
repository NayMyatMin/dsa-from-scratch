# 1295. Find Numbers with Even Number of Digits — Tier 4 (full strategy)

## The log trap, and which spelling actually has it

`math.log(x, 10)` is computed as a ratio of two natural logs, and the division does not always
round where you want it:

```python
>>> import math
>>> math.log(1000, 10)
2.9999999999999996
>>> int(math.log(1000, 10)) + 1
3                      # but 1000 has four digits
```

That is not a hypothetical. In `tests/ch01/test_p02_find_numbers_even_digits.py`, `([1000], 1)` is
an explicit parametrized case in `test_examples_and_edges`, `([999, 1000], 1)` is another, and
10^3 is one of the `boundaries` that `test_clusters_around_boundaries` builds its arrays from. You
will not sneak this past the suite. Swept over the whole legal domain 1..10^5,
`int(math.log(x, 10)) + 1` disagrees with `len(str(x))` on exactly one value — 1000. One wrong
answer in a hundred thousand is what makes it dangerous rather than merely broken.

`math.log10(x)` is a different function, not a shorthand for the same thing. Measured on this
build (CPython 3.14.4):

- Every value 1..10^5: `int(math.log10(x)) + 1 == len(str(x))`, **zero** mismatches.
- Every exact power of ten from 10^0 to 10^308: **zero** mismatches. `math.log10(1000)` is exactly
  `3.0`, and `math.log10(1000) == 3.0` is `True`.

So within this problem's constraints `math.log10` never trips. Its failures live somewhere else
entirely — just *below* a power of ten, starting at 10^k − 1 for k ≥ 15:

```python
>>> int(math.log10(10**15 - 1)) + 1
16                     # but 10**15 - 1 has fifteen digits
```

Scanning 10^k−1, 10^k, 10^k+1 for k < 300 turns up 285 mismatches and every single one of them is
a 10^k−1 value; no exact power of ten is ever wrong. That range is far outside `1 <= nums[i] <=
10^5`, so it cannot bite you here — but it is why the whole family still deserves the warning. The
log approach is the only one of the three that can be *silently* wrong, and whether it is wrong
depends on the spelling and on the domain. The honest interview sentence is not "log10 is broken";
it is "I used `math.log10`, not `math.log(x, 10)`, and I checked it holds over the stated
constraints."

Timed on this machine over 2000 random values in range, best of 10 runs: `math.log10` is the
fastest of the three, but only just — about 2% ahead of `len(str(x))`, run after run in the same
order. The divide-down loop is the clear loser at roughly 2.2x the log version. If you are
reaching for `log10` on speed grounds alone, that 2% is what you are buying with the correctness
caveat above.

## Now the one that matters

The constraints say `1 <= nums[i] <= 10^5`. That is a *tiny* domain — at most six digits.
Even-digit numbers are exactly:

    10 – 99          (2 digits)
    1000 – 9999      (4 digits)
    100000           (6 digits, and it's the only one)

So the digit count can be skipped entirely: three range checks, no division, no allocation, no
loop. O(1) per element with a very small constant. Measured against the same 2000 values, it comes
in at roughly half the time of the `len(str(x))` version.

This is the actual lesson. Constraints are not fine print — they are part of the problem
statement, and a tight bound is frequently an invitation to a different algorithm. Get in the
habit of reading them before you start.
