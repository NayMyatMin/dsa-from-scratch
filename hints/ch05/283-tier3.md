# 283. Move Zeroes — Tier 3 (approach)

Three families. All three are accepted by the suite and by the submission page, and they differ in
what they spend rather than in whether they are right — one of them allocates nothing, one allocates
a second copy of the data, and one pays a `log n` factor for a shorter program.

## Two indices, one reading and one writing

Tier 2 established that no surviving element ever ends up to the right of where it started. That is
a licence: it means the position you are about to write is a position you have already read past, so
writing into the list while you are still walking it cannot destroy anything you still need. One
index does the reading and moves every step; a second index marks where the next survivor belongs
and moves only when a survivor is found. The gap between them is the tally from Tier 2.

> **The official hint, verbatim** — the second of the two, and this is the tier it belongs to: "A
> *two-pointer* approach could be helpful here. The idea would be to have one pointer for iterating
> the array and another pointer that just works on the non-zero elements of the array."
>
> That is this family named and nothing more, which is the right amount for a hint and not enough to
> write from. Everything below is what it leaves you to decide.

Four decisions are left, and every one of them is somewhere this family goes wrong:

- **What happens to the positions past where the writing index stopped.** Tier 2 says how many there
  are and what has to be in them. Say when you put it there.
- **Copy the survivor forward, or exchange the two positions.** Both are correct and they are
  different programs; they differ in what they leave behind them and in how many slots they touch.
  Tier 4 counts that.
- **Whether to write at all when the two indices are equal.** This is the follow-up's question
  ("could you minimize the total number of operations done"), and it is the one decision here that
  cannot make you wrong, only busier.
- **Which index is allowed to be ahead of the other.** Answer it from Tier 2's sweep rather than by
  trying both and seeing which passes — one direction is safe for a reason, and the reason is the
  whole correctness argument.

Say what your version does to `[1, 2, 3]` before you run it. That input never moves anything, and a
version that mishandles it usually mishandles it silently.

## Build the survivors elsewhere, then write them back through the same object

The family the first official hint recommends starting from. Collect what survives into a list of
your own, pad it with as many zeros as you removed, and put the result into the list you were handed.
That last step is the whole of it, and it is where this family fails when it fails — chapter 1
section 9, and the comparison chapter 3 section 6 draws between a local name rebound and a write
that goes through the object:

```python
def rebuilt(buf):
    buf = [x * 2 for x in buf]       # a local name, rebound; the caller keeps the list it passed

def written_back(buf):
    buf[:] = [x * 2 for x in buf]    # the same object, its contents replaced

shelf = [3, 5, 8]
rebuilt(shelf)
print(shelf)
written_back(shelf)
print(shelf, len(shelf))
# -> [3, 5, 8]
# -> [6, 10, 16] 3
```

`rebuilt` does correct work and delivers it to an address nobody is watching. The suite has a
message for precisely that: a list that comes back exactly as it was handed over, when that is not
the answer, is reported as "nothing was written into `nums` at all". A slice assignment across the
whole list writes through the object, and because the replacement here is the same length as what it
replaces, `len` is unchanged.

This family is correct, it is accepted, and it is the quickest of the three. Measured on this repo's
interpreter, CPython 3.14.4, at n = 10^4 over six rounds on each of six zero patterns, the two-index
walk cost between 1.9x and 2.3x what this one cost, and the ordering held on all thirty-six
readings.

The reason is not that one route is running at C speed and the other is not — a comprehension is
bytecode per element the same as a loop. **The difference is the indexing.** Iterating a list hands
you each element; reaching for it by subscript is a separate operation, and doing it twice per
element is what the walk is paying for:

```python
import timeit

setup = "nums = [0 if i % 3 == 0 else i for i in range(10_000)]; n = len(nums)"
base = None
for stmt in ("for x in nums: pass",
             "for i in range(n): nums[i]",
             "for i in range(n): nums[i] = nums[i]"):
    best = min(timeit.repeat(stmt, setup, number=100, repeat=7)) / 100 * 1e6
    base = base or best
    print(f"{stmt:38s} {best:6.1f} us   {best / base:4.1f}x")
# -> for x in nums: pass                      24.1 us    1.0x
# -> for i in range(n): nums[i]               90.2 us    3.7x
# -> for i in range(n): nums[i] = nums[i]    109.5 us    4.5x
```

The microseconds move between runs and the ordering does not: over fifty-six runs the middle row
landed between 3.6x and 4.1x of the first and the last between 4.2x and 4.8x, cheapest to dearest in
that order every single time. The list is the same one in all three rows, so nothing about the
values is involved — this is the cost of the *form* of the access, and chapter 1 section 8 prices
the bounds check and the pointer offset that make it up.

What this family spends instead is memory, and how much depends on how many zeros there are: peak
traced allocation at n = 10^4 was 160,000 bytes on a list of nothing but zeros, 245,120 on a list
with none, and 201,824 to 201,920 on the four mixed patterns, against 0 for a walk that only writes
into slots that already exist. Every one of those figures came out about ten times larger at
n = 10^5 — 1,600,000, 2,400,928, and 2,044,320 to 2,044,416 — so it grows with the list. Nothing in
this suite measures that, and it is the subject of the chapter.

## A stable sort with a key

Sort by a key that is falsy for the survivors and truthy for the zeros. `False` sorts before `True`,
and Python's sort is stable — elements with equal keys come out in the order they went in — so every
survivor keeps its place relative to every other survivor, which is the half of this problem that is
hard to get right:

```python
pairs = [("c", 1), ("a", 0), ("b", 1), ("d", 0)]
print(pairs.sort(key=lambda p: p[1]), pairs)
# -> None [('a', 0), ('d', 0), ('c', 1), ('b', 1)]
```

Two things in one line. `('c', 1)` still precedes `('b', 1)` afterwards, which is stability doing the
work; and `.sort()` returned `None` while sorting the list in place, which is this problem's contract
exactly. `sorted()` is the other one — it leaves the original alone and hands back a new list, which
reaches nobody here.

Checked against the statement over all 5,460 lists of length 1 to 6 drawn from `{0, 1, 2, -1}`, this
family is wrong on 0 of them. It costs O(n log n) rather than O(n), and what it allocates says what
the sort is doing. Peak traced at n = 10^4: 80,168 bytes wherever the zeros form one block or there
are none, which is eight bytes an element and change — one pointer per computed key. Where the zeros
are scattered it is 120,168 and where they alternate with values 120,200, and that extra 40,000-odd
bytes is about 5,000 pointers, `n / 2` of them, which is the merge buffer chapter 1 section 8's sort
row prices at exactly that. Keys already in order give the sort a single run and nothing to merge;
interleaved keys do not.

Timed against it over thirty-six readings on six zero patterns, the walk cost between 0.54x and
1.02x of what this did, and the margin tracks the interleaving rather than the length: the sort cost
1.5x to 1.9x the walk where zeros and values alternate or are scattered through the list, 1.06x to
1.18x where they sit in one block, and on a list with no zeros in it the two came within a couple of
per cent of each other, which is not a gap worth explaining. It passes both guards comfortably.

## What not to reach for

Anything that changes the length. `pop`, `del`, `remove`, `insert` and a slice assignment of a
different length are all length changes, and the suite fails them on the length before it looks at
the contents. Three of them are also the quadratic trap Tier 2 counted: chapter 1 section 8 charges
`nums.pop(i)` and `del nums[i]` O(n − i), and `nums.remove(x)` O(n), so one per zero is a full-length
move inside a full-length loop.

And resist `nums.count(0)`, `0 in nums` and `nums.index(0)` inside the loop. Each is its own pass
over the list — the same table, the same reason — and everything they could tell you is already in
the tally you are keeping as you walk.
