# 1299. Replace Elements with Greatest Element on Right Side — Tier 3 (approach)

Three families. All three get the right answer and two of them are accepted by both timing guards;
what separates them is where the answers are assembled and what that costs.

## Carry the answer, and let each position hand it on

Tier 2 gave you a comparison that turns one answer into its neighbour. A traversal that visits the
positions in the order that makes the *known* side of that comparison the one it has already
computed never needs the list to be scanned twice. Which order that is falls straight out of which
of the two answers the comparison is written in terms of; say it out loud before you go on. Then one
number carried in a local variable is the whole of the state — you have done that before, in chapter
1's 485, where a running count travelled across a single scan.

Three decisions are left, and all three are where this family goes wrong.

**What the carried number starts as.** It is not the first element you look at. There is a position
whose answer the statement fixes outright rather than deriving, and the traversal above arrives at
that position first — so the value you start holding is written straight into the list, unexamined.
Say what it has to be, then check it against the constraints: `1 <= arr[i] <= 10^5`, so the value
the statement fixes sits below every value that can ever be in the list. Both of those facts are
load bearing and they are not the same fact.

**The order of the read and the write at each position.** Two things happen at every index: the
number you are carrying goes in, and the number that was there decides what you carry onwards. One
of those orders reads a value that the other one has already destroyed, and getting it backwards is
not a near miss — it is wrong on 1,089 of the 1,092 lists of length 1 to 6 over `{1, 2, 3}`, the
three survivors being the single-element lists. Its smallest failure is `[1, 1]`, which comes back
`[-1, -1]` instead of `[1, -1]`. The suite recognises the shape of that wreckage by name and says so
in its failure message rather than reporting an anonymous mismatch. Work it out on `[1, 1]` with a
pencil.

**Whether the last position needs its own branch.** Decide deliberately. If the first two decisions
are made well it does not, and neither does `len(arr) == 1`; if they are made badly, no amount of
branching at the end repairs them.

## Assemble the answers somewhere else, then write them in

Nothing forbids working the whole answer out in a list of its own — a list of suffix maxima built
right to left, or `accumulate` over the same nesting Tier 2 described, done by a C-level loop — and
then putting it where it belongs. The suite does not measure allocations and says so;
`notes/03_deleting.md` section 6 is the contract this problem is a gentle version of, where the
caller keeps its own reference and the length may not change.

"Putting it where it belongs" is the part that is checked, and it is one line whose two spellings do
entirely different things:

```python
def rebind(arr):
    arr = [8, 8]
    return arr


def rewrite(arr):
    arr[:] = [8, 8]
    return arr


caller = [1, 2]
returned = rebind(caller)
print(returned, caller, returned is caller)
# -> [8, 8] [1, 2] False

caller = [1, 2]
returned = rewrite(caller)
print(returned, caller, returned is caller)
# -> [8, 8] [8, 8] True
```

`arr = ...` moves your local label and leaves the caller's list untouched; `arr[:] = ...` overwrites
the elements of the object itself, and at equal lengths it is O(n) with no tail sliding anywhere —
`notes/01_introduction.md` section 8 has the row, and section 9 is the whole story of why a name
tells you nothing about how many other names are bound to the same object. The suite tests
`returned is arr`, not `returned == expected`, so a perfect fresh list fails. That check is the
suite's own addition rather than the problem's, and it is the suite that says so, in the docstring at
the top of `tests/ch05/test_p01_replace_greatest_right.py`: a submission is read for the array it
hands back and never for the one it was handed, so the fresh list is accepted there and fails here.
The stub states the rule; that file is where the reason it is a house rule is written down.

What this family costs, measured on this repo's interpreter, CPython 3.14.4, at `n = 10^4` over four
shapes, five rounds on each shape in each of three runs, with the routes timed one after the other
inside every round. **On time this family and the carried number are level, and what decides a
reading is not which route it is but how the loop is written**: the gap between two spellings of one
route is wider than the gap between the routes. A walk that carries one number has to index, because
it writes as it goes; a walk that only reads is free not to, and iterating the elements rather than
the positions is worth 1.3x to 1.5x on this loop — which is exactly what pays for the second list,
the reversal and the write-back. Spelled that way, building the answers and writing them back ran
0.86x to 1.04x the cost of carrying one number in place, over all sixty readings. Spelled with the
indexed loop the carried number has no choice about, the same route on the same answers ran 1.22x to
1.37x, slower on every shape in every reading. Quote either figure without saying which spelling
produced it and you have said nothing. The `accumulate` route was slower than the carried number on
every shape and in every one of the sixty readings, at 1.33x to 1.79x: a C-level loop is not
automatically the quicker route when it also builds a suffix list, turns part of it around and
writes the result back. That last comparison has a spelling behind it too, and it is the carried
number's — folding the running maximum with a comparison rather than with a call to `max()` moves
that route by 1.58x to 2.02x on its own, wider than either gap above and enough to turn both of them
around.

Memory is the part that does not move. Peak traced by `tracemalloc` over the call alone, identical
on every pass: at `n = 1,000`, 16,800 bytes for the hand-built list and 24,856 for `accumulate`
written as one accumulation and one reversed slice assignment, which is the spelling those bytes
belong to; at the ceiling, 165,120 and 245,176. Those grow with `n`, which is the one thing carrying
a single number never does.

## Walk the other way, and go looking only when you have to

The order the first family settled on is not the only legal one. **Writing at position `i` destroys
a value only the positions to the *left* of `i` still need, so a walk that goes left to right never
destroys anything it has not already used** — when it writes into `i`, everything from `i + 1`
onward is still exactly as it arrived. Such a walk can carry the greatest of the part it has not
reached yet, and it has to notice one thing the other direction never does: with every step, an
element leaves the region that maximum is over. When the element leaving is the value being carried,
what is carried may not be there any more, and the honest repair is to go and find the greatest of
what is left.

It is correct — 0 wrong over those same 1,092 lists — and it is the family the growth guard was
built to catch. What it costs is decided by the input rather than by the algorithm:

| shape | eight times the input, four readings | per call at `n = 10^4`, against one carried number |
|---|---|---|
| strictly ascending | 8.31x to 8.37x | 1.9x to 2.0x |
| random values over 1..10^5 | 7.92x to 7.99x | 2.1x to 2.2x |
| every element equal | 61.38x to 64.57x | 2,967x to 3,135x |
| strictly descending | 64.87x to 66.15x | 2,252x to 2,565x |

On a strictly ascending list the greatest element is the last one, so nothing leaves that is worth
rescanning for until there is nothing left; on a strictly descending one the element leaving is the
greatest of what remains at every single step, and a list of one repeated value ties at every step
for the same reason. The suite's fifth timed shape, non-increasing with ties, measured 64.96x to
66.31x over the same four readings. The guard's ceiling is 25x. Run this on ascending or random data
alone and you will conclude it is linear. Word the trigger slightly differently and you move which
shapes expose it without fixing anything, which is why the guard times five of them and marks in its
docstring which wrong solutions each shape is blind to.

> **The official hints, verbatim** — there are two, and both belong to this tier: "Loop through the
> array starting from the end." and "Keep the maximum value seen so far."
>
> That is the first family, stated in eight words and then seven, and it is worth noticing how
> much of this tier they leave out. They do not say what the maximum "so far" is before anything
> has been seen, which is the first decision above and is not answerable from the phrase itself.
> They do not say whether the number written at a position is the maximum before or after that
> position's own value has been folded in, which is the second decision and the difference between
> a right answer and 1,089 wrong ones. They say nothing about the position whose answer the
> statement fixes rather than derives, and nothing about what has to come back to the caller —
> which, in this suite, is half the contract.
