# 905. Sort Array By Parity — Tier 4 (full strategy)

## Two positions, converging

Keep two indices: `i` at 0 and `j` at the last index. Loop while `i` is less than `j`, and each turn
ask at most two questions in this order.

- **Is `nums[i]` even?** Then it is already in front of everything `j` has passed and everything
  still between them, and it never needs looking at again. Move `i` on by one and take the next
  turn.
- **Otherwise, is `nums[j]` odd?** The same argument from the other end. Move `j` back by one.
- **Otherwise** `nums[i]` is odd and `nums[j]` is even. Neither is where it belongs, and each is
  standing exactly where the other belongs — so **exchange the two, then move `i` on and `j` back,
  because one exchange has settled both of them.**

When the loop ends, return `nums`. **The signature says `list[int]` and the suite grades what comes
back: rearranging the list beautifully and returning nothing fails.** Nothing looks at `nums`
afterwards, so returning the same object you were handed is a choice about space, not a contract.

## Why it is correct

One invariant carries the whole argument: **every index below `i` holds an even value and every
index above `j` holds an odd one.** It is true at the start, vacuously, because there are no such
indices. Each of the three branches is exactly the test that keeps it true — the first extends the
even region by an element it has just checked is even, the second extends the odd region the same
way, and the third makes both true at once by putting an even value at `i` and an odd one at `j`
before moving either.

Each turn moves `i` forward, or `j` back, or both, so the gap closes and the loop ends with `i` and
`j` either meeting or crossing. If they crossed, every index is below `i` or above `j` and the
invariant says the list reads as evens then odds. If they met, one element — the one at `i` — has
never been examined, and it needs no examining: everything below it is even and everything above it
is odd, so if it is even it is the last of the evens and if it is odd it is the first of the odds.
The list reads correctly either way, and that is why there is no `len(nums) < 2` branch anywhere and
no special case for an odd length.

Checked rather than argued: over all 87,380 lists of length 1 to 8 drawn from `{0, 1, 2, 3}`, this
shape produced an accepted answer every time, with no length branch and no handling of the middle
element. Writing the loop as `while i <= j` instead is also correct on all 87,380 — with `<=` the
meeting element does get examined, and whichever of the first two branches fires ends the loop.
Either bound works; pick one deliberately rather than by accident.

## Complexity, and what it actually costs

`i` starts at 0 and `j` at `n - 1`, and between them they have `n - 1` steps of travel before they
meet, with every turn spending at least one. So the loop runs at most `n - 1` turns and performs at
most `n // 2` exchanges. Counted rather than argued: over those same 87,380 lists the turn count
never exceeded `n - 1` and the exchange count never exceeded `n // 2`, and both bounds were reached.
**O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** `tracemalloc` over the whole routine at n = 5,000: **0 bytes** peak
on all 100 traced passes, and 0 again at n = 10,000 and n = 20,000 — nothing that grows with `n`.

Measured on this repo's interpreter, CPython 3.14.4, at n = 5,000, best of fifteen batched rounds
with every shape and every route timed in rotation rather than one after another, over three runs,
on five of the eleven shapes the suite builds: 0.123 - 0.124 ms per call on a list of nothing but
even values, 0.150 - 0.152 ms on one built odds-first, 0.157 - 0.158 ms on one already arranged
evens-then-odds, 0.188 - 0.191 ms on a list of nothing but odd values and 0.202 - 0.203 ms on a
random mix. The random mix was the dearest shape for every correct family measured, in all three
runs, here and below.

The gap between the two single-parity shapes is not about work done — neither performs a single
exchange, and both take exactly `n - 1` turns, 4,999 of them at this size. It is worth knowing why,
because it is a property of the code and not of the machine. With the branches in the order given
above, every turn on the all-odd list asks both questions and every turn on the all-even list asks
one: 9,998 parity tests against 4,999, counted rather than estimated. Swap the two tests and that
ordering swaps with them: measured over the same three runs, the all-even shape went from
0.123 - 0.124 ms to 0.190 - 0.191 and the all-odd shape from 0.188 - 0.191 ms to 0.122 - 0.124,
while the random mix moved by 1% or less and stayed the dearest under either order. Both orders are
correct — the swapped one answers all 87,380 lists above correctly too — and both are linear.
Neither is worth choosing on those numbers.

Against the suite's growth guard — 4,000 elements to 32,000, eight times the input — this shape
measured 7.96x to 8.20x on all four shapes that guard times, three readings of each, where its
ceiling is 25x.

## The approaches not taken

**Two groups, then one list.** One pass, each value appended to an evens list or an odds list, the
two joined. Measured 0.119 to 0.173 ms per call at n = 5,000 and growth of 8.11x to 8.46x. On four
of the five shapes it beat the converging walk in every one of the three runs, the walk costing
1.17x to 1.58x of it — worst on the all-odd list, the shape where the walk takes the full `n - 1`
turns and asks both questions in every one of them. The fifth shape decides nothing: on a list of nothing but even values, where the walk asks one
question a turn and exchanges nothing, the two measured 0.119 - 0.126 ms against 0.123 - 0.124 ms
and which of them came out ahead changed from run to run. What the two groups cost is space:
80,960 to 81,824 bytes peak traced at n = 5,000, 163,648 to 165,120 at n = 10,000 and 330,240 to
332,960 at n = 20,000. **So the in-place walk buys space and not time, and this problem does not ask
for either.** Chapter 1 section 9 is the reason the choice is even available: `nums` is a list the
caller still holds a name for, and rearranging it where it stands and building a new one are two
different things happening to two different objects.

**The same two groups, as two comprehensions.** 0.172 to 0.270 ms per call, growth 7.95x to 8.40x,
the same peak bytes. It reads the list twice and computes every parity twice, and shape for shape it
was the slower of the two group-building routes by 40% to 58%, on all five shapes in all three runs.
It is also the shortest correct thing here after the sort.

**A sort with a key on parity.** 0.128 to 0.217 ms per call, peak traced 80,168 bytes on the four
patterned shapes and 100,056 on the random mix, growth 8.05x to 8.69x. Accepted everywhere, and
the only family here that is O(n log n) by contract; the log factor is not visible at these sizes.
Its two risks are in Tier 3 and neither is about parity.

**Carrying one group across the list an element at a time.** This is the shape the chapter exists to
catch, and its cost depends entirely on which parity the input is made of, so no single input
convicts it. Inserting each even at the front measured 3.66 - 3.67 ms per call on a list of nothing
but even values and 0.109 - 0.114 ms on a list of nothing but odd ones, growth 60.45x - 60.99x on
the first and 8.18x - 8.30x on the second. Carrying each odd to the back one at a time is the mirror
image: 0.110 - 0.111 ms and growth 8.00x - 8.06x on the all-even list, 2.06 ms and growth
96.20x - 97.63x on the all-odd one. On a random mix both are caught, at 57.06x - 57.33x and
80.55x - 80.88x against a ceiling of 25x. Either single-parity shape on its own would report one of
the two as linear, which is why `test_does_not_grow_quadratically` times four.

Note what the ceiling budget does *not* do here. The dearest of those readings at n = 5,000 is
3.67 ms a call, and `test_constraint_ceiling` allows 3.000 s for 500 calls — six milliseconds each,
so 500 of those calls would spend about 1.83 s of a 3.00 s budget and pass comfortably. The budget
is built that way on purpose. Its own docstring sets it at 31x the slowest of the ten
straightforward correct solutions measured for it, and of the six slower routes it tabulates, four
come in under it — every one of those four quadratic. **The ceiling is a floor, not a filter, and
the growth guard is the test that sees algorithm shape.** A solution can be quadratic, comfortable
at the real ceiling, and accepted by the judge, and still be the mistake.

## Before you call it done

Run `uv run pytest tests/ch05/test_p03_sort_array_by_parity.py`, then check by hand: `[0]`, `[1]`,
`[2]`, `[1, 2]`, `[2, 1]`, `[1, 3]`, `[2, 4]`, `[0, 1, 3]`, `[1, 3, 0]`, `[5000, 0]`,
`[4999, 5000]` and `[1, 0, 0, 3, 0]`. Six of those twelve have more than one accepted answer —
`[1, 3]`, `[2, 4]`, `[0, 1, 3]`, `[1, 3, 0]`, `[5000, 0]` and `[1, 0, 0, 3, 0]` — so an output that
looks wrong to you may not be. Check each one against the rule rather than against the answer you
had in mind.

Confirm you return a list. Both routes end differently and both have to end with one: a fresh list
is returned as itself, and a rearranged `nums` still needs an explicit `return nums`. Confirm you
did not sort by value, which is a different question and is rejected by name. Confirm you did not
quietly promise an order inside either group — if your answer keeps the evens in arrival order that
is fine, but nothing should depend on it and nothing should cost you anything to achieve it.

And confirm you treat `0` as even. It is the value most often mishandled here, the suite gives it a
block of six hand-written cases of its own, and `0 % 2` is `0`, the same as every other even value.
