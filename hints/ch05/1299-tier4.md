# 1299. Replace Elements with Greatest Element on Right Side — Tier 4 (full strategy)

## One pass from the right, carrying one number

Keep one integer — call it `largest` — and start it at `-1`. **Visit the indices from the last one
down to 0, and at each index do three things in this order: take a copy of the value that is there,
write `largest` into that slot, then let `largest` become the larger of `largest` and the value you
copied.** When the loop ends, return `arr` itself.

That is the whole algorithm, and each of the three steps is doing a job the statement gave it.

**The seed does double duty, and that is why it must be exactly `-1`.** It is the first thing
written, into the last slot, and the statement fixes that slot's answer at `-1` outright rather than
deriving it. It is also the starting value of a running maximum, and it has to lose every comparison
it ever takes part in — which it does, because `1 <= arr[i] <= 10^5` puts every legal element above
it. Neither half is optional and neither can be swapped for something more natural-looking. Seeding
with `0` instead is wrong on all 1,092 lists of length 1 to 6 over `{1, 2, 3}`, because the seed is
literally what lands in the final slot; seeding with `float("-inf")` leaves `-inf` sitting there,
and is wrong on all 1,092 for the same reason.

**Copy before you overwrite.** The value at the index you are standing on is the one element that
separates this position's answer from the answer of the position to its left, and writing first
destroys it — after the write, what is in the slot is the number you just put there. It is the last
moment that value exists. Getting this backwards is wrong on 1,089 of those same 1,092 lists, the
three survivors being the single-element ones, and its smallest failure is `[1, 1]` coming back as
`[-1, -1]` instead of `[1, -1]`. That wreckage has a signature the suite recognises: every position
`-1`, because the `-1` written into the last slot becomes the largest thing the walk has seen and
then propagates all the way to the front.

Iterate by index rather than by element. You need to write at a position, not merely read one, and
the loop's whole subject is the slot it is standing on.

Two things then fall out rather than being handled. There is no branch for the last index: the
traversal arrives there first, holding the seed, and writes the answer the statement fixed. And
there is no branch for `len(arr) == 1`: that list *is* the last index and nothing else, so the same
first step finishes it. Checked with no length branch and no end-of-list special case anywhere, this
shape agrees with the statement on all 1,092 of those lists, three of which have a single element.

## Why one number is enough

Tier 2's observation is the correctness argument and there is nothing else to it. The elements to
the right of `i` are the element at `i + 1` together with the elements to the right of `i + 1`, so
the greatest of them is the greater of two numbers — and one of those two is the answer already
computed for `i + 1`. **Travelling from the right means the answer for `i + 1` is always the last
thing the loop worked out, so it can live in a variable instead of being searched for.** Verified as
a bare identity, not as code: over those 1,092 lists, at all 4,923 positions that have a neighbour
to the right, the answer never once differed from `max(arr[i + 1], answer[i + 1])`.

The invariant, if you want it in one sentence: when the loop is about to write at index `i`,
`largest` holds the greatest of the values `arr` originally held at `i + 1` and beyond, or `-1` when
there are none. The write makes that the answer at `i`; folding in the copied value re-establishes
it for `i - 1`.

## Complexity, and what it actually costs

Every index is visited once and does a fixed amount of work: one comparison, one write. Counted
rather than argued — one comparison and one write on a single-element list, six and six on
`[17, 18, 5, 4, 6, 1]`, and 10,000 and 10,000 at the constraint ceiling on both a strictly
descending list and a strictly ascending one. **O(n) time, O(1) extra space.** `tracemalloc` over
the call alone, four passes at each of `n = 1,000`, `n = 8,000` and `n = 10^4`: **0 bytes** peak on
every pass but the first one taken in a fresh process, which reports a few dozen bytes of
`tracemalloc`'s own start-up and does not even reproduce — 40 bytes in one process and 72 in the
next, then 0 for the rest of its life. Nothing is allocated because nothing is built.

One choice inside that loop is worth more than any ratio in the next section, and it is how the fold
is spelled. Write it as a comparison — test the copied value against `largest`, assign only when it
wins — and the same walk written with `max()` instead cost 1.58x to 2.02x as much, over sixty
readings across four shapes and three runs, the two timed one after the other inside every round.
**Every figure below was measured with the comparison spelling.** Measure them against the `max()`
spelling and they do not merely shift: the two routes that come nearest below, a second list and
`accumulate`, both turn into the quicker one.

Measured on this repo's interpreter, CPython 3.14.4, at `n = 10^4` over four shapes — strictly
descending, strictly ascending, random values over the full range, and `[7] * n` — with three
independent sets of rounds: 0.127 to 0.195 ms per call, a copy of the input included in every
reading. The counted work is identical on all four and the readings are not: `[7] * n` was the
quickest in every round of every run, 0.127 ms against 0.138 for the ascending list. That is a
property of the list rather than of the walk, and it is worth knowing *which* property, because it
is not "the values are equal". `[7] * n` is one integer object with `n` pointers to it, so every
read in the walk lands on the same object. Built instead as `n` distinct objects that happen to hold
equal values, the same shape measured 0.138 to 0.139 ms — level with the ascending list, and which
of those two was quicker changed between runs. There is no early exit here and no input this walk
does less work on. Against the suite's own guards: its 20 calls at the ceiling took 2.83 to 2.88 ms
over eight rounds in each of three runs, inside the 250 ms budget by a factor of 86, and eight times
the input cost between 8.26x and 8.80x the time over five shapes and two readings each, against a
ceiling of 25x.

## The approaches not taken

**Assemble the answers in a list of your own, then write them back.** Walk from the right appending
each answer, turn the result around, and put it into `arr` with `arr[:] = out`. It is accepted — the
suite grades where the answer ends up, not how it was reached — and on speed there is nothing to
choose, though only once you say how both loops are written. This route never writes into `arr`
during the walk, so unlike the carried number it is free to iterate the elements rather than the
positions, and that freedom alone is worth 1.3x to 1.5x — enough to pay for the second list, the
reversal and the write-back. Iterating elements it ran 0.86x to 1.04x the carried number over sixty
readings, and its 20 ceiling calls took 2.77 to 2.89 ms against that route's 2.83 to 2.88.
Iterating positions instead — the same route, the same answers, one line spelled differently — it
ran 1.22x to 1.37x, slower on every shape in every reading, and its ceiling calls took 3.67 to 3.94
ms. **The gap between the two spellings is wider than any gap between the shapes, so no shape
decides this and neither, on its own, does the route.** Growth came out 7.29x to 8.50x across both
spellings. What does not depend on any of it is memory: peak traced 16,800 bytes at `n = 1,000`,
131,168 at `n = 8,000` and 165,120 at `n = 10^4`, against 0 at all three. That figure grows with
`n`. Nothing in this suite measures space and the constraints do not force it, so **constant extra
space is the better habit here rather than a requirement of the problem** — which is exactly why
this problem is the gentlest place in the chapter to build it.

**`accumulate` over the reversed list with `max`, then the same write-back.** Tier 2's nesting,
done by a C-level loop. It was the slower route on every shape and in every one of the sixty
readings: 1.33x to 1.79x the cost of carrying one number, 20 ceiling calls in 4.50 to 4.81 ms,
growth 7.23x to 8.17x, and — written as one accumulation and one reversed slice assignment, which is
the spelling those bytes belong to — peak traced memory of 24,856, 195,224 and 245,176 bytes at the
three sizes. Running at C speed does not pay for building a suffix list, turning part of it around
and writing the result into `arr`. It is worth knowing `accumulate` exists and worth not reaching
for it reflexively. Notice what it is losing to, though: against the `max()` spelling of the carried
walk rather than the comparison one, this route is the quicker of the two on every shape, at 0.74x
to 0.98x. What wins here is settled inside the loop body, not by which route it is.

**Walk left to right instead, carrying the greatest of the part you have not reached yet, and go
looking again whenever the element that leaves is the value you were carrying.** Writing at `i`
destroys only what the positions left of `i` needed, and they are already done, so this direction is
legal in place too. It is correct on all 1,092 lists, and it is the thing the growth guard was built
to catch, because what it costs is decided by the input rather than by the algorithm:

| shape | eight times the input, four readings | per call at `n = 10^4`, against one carried number |
|---|---|---|
| strictly ascending | 8.31x to 8.37x | 1.9x to 2.0x |
| random values over 1..10^5 | 7.92x to 7.99x | 2.1x to 2.2x |
| every element equal | 61.38x to 64.57x | 2,967x to 3,135x |
| strictly descending | 64.87x to 66.15x | 2,252x to 2,565x |

The suite's fifth timed shape, non-increasing with ties, measured 64.96x to 66.31x over the same
four readings. Its 20 calls at the ceiling took 4,764 and 4,839 ms in two runs, over the budget by
nineteen times at its quickest. A list of one repeated value is its worst case rather than its best,
because every element that leaves ties the value being carried. Reword the trigger and the quadratic
shapes move somewhere else without anything being fixed, which is why the guard times five shapes
and its docstring marks which wrong solutions each shape is blind to.

**`max()` over a fresh slice, once per position.** This is what the suite's reference computes —
`tests/_reference/p01_replace_greatest_right.py`, the statement transcribed with nothing added,
where being obviously right is the entire job and speed is not part of it. Timed with a write-back
bolted on so it can be compared with the rest: 412 to 470 ms per call at `n = 10^4` across the four
shapes, 2,225x to 2,996x the carried number, eight times the input costing 63.09x to 67.60x, and
9.0 seconds for the 20 ceiling calls that have 250 ms to finish in. Every slice it takes is also a
fresh list of pointers, so the copying grows the same way the reading does.

**`max()` over a generator, once per position.** The same algorithm with the copying removed, and
slower still: 1,111 to 1,228 ms per call, 6,101x to 7,958x, growth 63.32x to 65.33x, and 22.4
seconds over the 20 ceiling calls. The growth is the same either way, which is the point — it was
never the copying that made this shape unaffordable.

## Before you call it done

Run `uv run pytest tests/ch05/test_p01_replace_greatest_right.py`, then check by hand: `[400]` gives
`[-1]`, `[1, 1]` gives `[1, -1]`, `[5, 4, 3]` gives `[4, 3, -1]`, `[1, 2, 3]` gives `[3, 3, -1]`,
`[7, 7, 7]` gives `[7, 7, -1]`, `[1, 9, 9, 1]` gives `[9, 9, 1, -1]`, `[100, 1, 2, 3]` gives
`[3, 3, 3, -1]`, and `[17, 18, 5, 4, 6, 1]` gives `[18, 6, 6, 6, 1, -1]`.

Then three contract checks, because this problem asks for both halves and the suite reads both.
Confirm that what you return *is* `arr` — `returned is arr`, not a fresh list with the same contents
and not `None`. Confirm `len(arr)` is what it was: no `append`, no `insert`, no `pop`, no `del`, and
no slice assignment that changes the count. And confirm the last slot is `-1` on every input,
including the one-element one, which is the whole of that case.

Finally, resist sorting. The question is entirely about which elements sit to the right of which,
and `arr.sort()` destroys that before you can ask.
