# 905. Sort Array By Parity — Tier 3 (approach)

Three families, all accepted by the suite and by the judge. They differ in what they allocate and in
how many ways each one can be got wrong, not in what they cost per element.

## Two groups, then one list

One pass over `nums`, sending each value to one of two lists according to its parity, and the two
joined for the answer. Nothing is ever put anywhere but the end of a list, so Tier 2's expensive
operation never appears. It is the plainest reading of the observation — the answer is the evens
then the odds, so make the evens, make the odds, put them in that order.

It is a correct answer to this problem. The suite never looks at `nums` after the call and nothing
here measures space, and the docstring says so in as many words. What it costs is the space:
measured on this repo's interpreter, CPython 3.14.4, peak traced memory at n = 5,000 was 80,960 to
81,824 bytes across the shapes measured, and it grows with the input — on a random mix, 163,648
bytes at n = 10,000 and 330,240 at n = 20,000. That is one pointer per element, twice over.

One decision inside this family. Building the two groups in a single loop measured 0.119 to 0.173 ms
per call at n = 5,000 over five of the suite's shapes; building them as two separate comprehensions,
one filtering for evens and one for odds, measured 0.172 to 0.270 ms. Read those bands shape by
shape rather than end to end: the second reads the list twice and tests every element's parity
twice, and on every one of those five shapes, in each of three runs, it was the slower of the two by
between 40% and 58%. It is also shorter, and "just shorter" is a real reason as long as you know
which one you are picking.

## Hand it to a sort, with a key

`sorted` accepts a `key` function, and a key whose value depends only on an element's parity puts
every element into one of two buckets and orders the buckets. It is the shortest route by a
distance, it is accepted, and it is the only family here that is not linear by its own contract.
Measured 0.128 to 0.217 ms per call at n = 5,000 over the same five shapes; against the growth
guard, 4,000 elements to 32,000, it measured 8.05x to 8.69x, which is what the linear families
measured too. The `log` factor in its contract is real and is not visible at these sizes.

Two ways to get this one wrong, and neither of them is about parity:

```python
nums = [3, 1, 2, 4]
print(sorted(nums), nums.sort(), nums)
# -> [1, 2, 3, 4] None [1, 2, 3, 4]
```

Sorting by value is a different question and the suite rejects it by name. And `sorted` builds a new
list while `nums.sort()` rearranges `nums` and hands back `None` — this problem grades the return
value, so the second of those fails however well it rearranged the list.

One thing this family gives you without being asked: Python's sort is stable, so values keep their
arrival order within their group. Nothing requires that. Do not pay anything extra to get it and do
not build a solution that leans on it.

## Rearranging `nums` where it stands

Two positions, one starting at each end, moving towards each other. Each one advances for as long as
what it is looking at already belongs on its own side. When both are stuck at once, the two values
they are stuck on are each on the wrong side of the boundary — and each is standing exactly where
the other belongs, so a single exchange settles both and both positions move on. The walk is over
when the two positions meet.

Nothing is inserted and nothing is removed, so the length never changes and the only edit in play is
indexed assignment — the length-preserving family chapter 3 section 6 works within. Measured peak
traced memory at n = 5,000: **0 bytes**, on all 100 traced passes, and 0 again at n = 10,000 and
n = 20,000 — nothing that grows with `n`, against the 80,960-plus of a route that builds a list.

This is why 905 sits in this chapter, and it is worth doing for that reason alone. It is not what
the problem asks for. **The statement, the suite and the judge all accept an answer built from
scratch, and nothing anywhere checks what happened to `nums`** — reach for this because it is the
better exercise, not because anything is holding you to it.

Three things are left for you, and they are where this family goes wrong:

- The loop condition. Say what happens when the two positions land on the same index, and whether
  that element has to be looked at.
- What moves after an exchange. One position, or both.
- What bounds each advance. A position that keeps moving for as long as it likes what it sees needs
  the other position as a stopping point as well, and without that the walk runs off the end: on
  `[2, 4, 6, 8]` it raises `IndexError: list index out of range` rather than answering.

> **The official hints, verbatim** — the record for this problem reads, in full: *Official hints:
> none.* That is worth a sentence rather than none at all. Hints exist where a statement hides its
> difficulty, and this one hides nothing: everything it is prepared to tell you is in "return any
> array that satisfies this condition", and that sentence is a grant rather than a clue. It is also
> the sentence most often read past, which is the trap here — inventing a requirement nobody stated
> and paying for it.

There is a fourth family, and it is the one the rest of this chapter is built around. It is
deliberately not sketched here: meeting it for the first time on a problem that does not need it
would spend it for nothing.
