# 941. Valid Mountain Array — Tier 3 (approach)

Three families. All three are accepted by both guards in the suite, and the differences between
them are in how easy each one is to get wrong, not in how fast it runs.

## Locate the summit first, then check the two sides of it

Tier 2's first observation hands you this one: a mountain's summit is the strict maximum, so
`max(arr)` and `.index()` between them find the only candidate the list has. Reading the list three
or four times over is still O(n), and this family pays for its simplicity in passes rather than in
growth.

Do not price those extra passes at zero because the loop inside them runs at C speed. At
`n = 10^4` on this repo's interpreter, CPython 3.14.4, `max(arr)` measured 71 us, and the same
maximum found by a Python-level loop over the same list measured 54 us: the builtin was the slower
of the two by about a third, and that ordering held over five rounds and under two separate ways of
timing it. What `.index()` buys is not speed per element but the right to stop early — on a summit
sitting in the middle of the list it reads half the elements and measured 28 us.

Two decisions are left, and both of them are where this family goes wrong:

```python
readings = [3, 7, 9, 9, 4, 1]
print(readings.index(max(readings)), max(readings))
# -> 2 9
```

`.index()` hands back the *first* occurrence, so on a plateau at the summit you get a position the
list does not strictly fall away from. That is survivable — a strict check on the right-hand side
rejects it — and it is only survivable if the check is strict. A version of this family that
verifies the two sides with `<=` and `>=` is wrong on 759 of the 5,460 lists of length 1 to 6 over
`{0, 1, 2, 3}`, and its smallest failure is three elements long: `[0, 1, 1]` comes back True.

The other decision is the fence from Tier 1. Say what you do when the candidate lands at index 0
or at the last index, and say it before you look at either side.

Verifying the two sides must be one pass each. One pass *per candidate* is Tier 2's twelve million
comparisons wearing a different hat.

## Walk the steps once, and stop where the sign changes

Start at the left and keep moving while the step in front of you is a climb. Where you stop is the
only candidate the list has, and you did not search for it — the walk manufactured it. Left for
you:

- Two questions about *where* you stopped, both of which can reject the list before the rest of it
  is worth reading. Tier 1's fence is one of them; the other is what "there was a climb at all"
  means as a test on an index.
- What you then demand of the rest of the list, and where the second phase starts — on the index
  the first phase stopped at, or one past it. Those are different programs. Pick one deliberately.

The two questions about where you stopped are not decoration. Over those same 5,460 lists, a walk
that asks neither is wrong on 26 of them, and the smallest failure is a single element: `[0]`
answered True. A walk that asks only whether the climb happened is wrong on 11, smallest failure
`[0, 1]`. And a walk that uses `<=` and `>=` instead of `<` and `>` is wrong on 759, smallest
failure `[1, 1, 0]`. Three separate mistakes, three separate sets of inputs, none of them exotic.

## Both ends inward

The symmetric version. Climb from the left while the steps rise; climb from the right while the
steps rise as you go backwards. Then accept only if the two walks stopped on the same index, and
neither of them stopped where it started.

This family costs the same and reads the same list twice, and it earns its place by making the
three conditions separate and visible: *met in the middle*, *the left one moved*, *the right one
moved*. Drop any one of them and you have one of the three failure sets above. Some people find
three explicit tests easier to get right than the same three folded into a single walk; some find
it easier to forget one.

There is a fourth route worth knowing about but not worth reaching for: slice the list at the
summit and check each half on its own. It is accepted here and by the judge, and what it costs is
measured in Tier 4.

> **The official hint, verbatim** — there is only one for this problem, and this is the tier it
> belongs to: "It's very easy to keep track of a monotonically increasing or decreasing ordering of
> elements. You just need to be able to determine the start of the valley in the mountain and from
> that point onwards, it should be a valley i.e. no mini-hills after that."
>
> That is the second family, pointed at from the far side: find where the descent begins, then
> insist nothing rises again. It is weaker than this tier in exactly the two places wrong answers
> live. It never says the summit has to be strictly inside the list, so it has nothing to say about
> a list that only climbs or one that only falls — and every one of the 26 above is exactly that:
> 11 that only climb, 11 that only fall, and the 4 one-element lists that have no steps at all. The
> 11 are not a second set but the only-climbing eleven inside the 26; asking whether the climb
> happened at all disposes of the other 15 and leaves those untouched. And it never mentions equal
> neighbours, which is the failure this problem is built around: a plateau at the summit has no
> mini-hills after the descent starts either. Its vocabulary works against it too — it calls the
> falling side "the valley", so "the start of the valley" means the summit, not the foot.
