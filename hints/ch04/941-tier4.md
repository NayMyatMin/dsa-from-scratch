# 941. Valid Mountain Array — Tier 4 (full strategy)

## One walk, two phases

Keep one integer, `i`, starting at 0. **Phase one: while there is a step in front of you and it is
a climb, move `i` on by one.** When that stops, one of two things is true — either `i` is the last
index, or the step out of `i` is not a climb. Nothing else can have ended the loop.

Now two rejections, and each one is a clause of the definition rather than a patch on the code:

- **`i` is still 0.** The very first step was not a climb, so nothing has a strictly rising run
  behind it and no index can play the summit. That is `arr[0] < arr[1] < ... < arr[i]` failing at
  its first term, and it disposes of `[3, 2, 1]`, `[5, 5]`, `[2, 1]` and `[7]` in one line.
- **`i` is the last index.** The climb ran off the end of the list and there is nothing left to
  fall to. That is `0 < i < arr.length - 1` failing on its right-hand side, and it disposes of
  `[0, 1, 2, 3]` and `[1, 2]`.

**Phase two: from that same `i`, keep moving while the step in front of you is a fall.** The answer
is whether `i` finishes on the last index. If phase two stopped early, the step that stopped it
was a tie or a climb — a plateau or a second summit — and both are fatal, so nothing more needs
asking. Return that comparison's result, which is already a real `bool`.

Iterate by index rather than by element here. The walk's whole subject is *where* it stopped, and
both rejections are statements about an index.

Two things fall out rather than being handled. There is no `len(arr) < 3` branch anywhere: a
one-element list cannot move `i` at all and the first rejection takes it, and a two-element list
either cannot move `i` or moves it onto the last index, so one of the two rejections takes that
too. Checked with no length branch present, this shape agrees with the definition on all 5,460
lists of length 1 to 6 drawn from `{0, 1, 2, 3}` — 20 of which are shorter than three elements.

And ties need no branch either. Every comparison in the definition is strict, so a flat step is
neither a climb nor a fall: it stops phase one, and if it sits past the summit it stops phase two.
Both stops are rejections, which is the whole of the plateau problem. Reach for `<=` and `>=`
instead and the shape is wrong on 759 of those 5,460 lists, its smallest failure `[1, 1, 0]`
answered True.

## Why one candidate is enough

The walk never chooses between candidate summits, and Tier 2 is why: at most one index can satisfy
the definition, because everything after a qualifying `i` strictly falls, so a later `j` arrives on
a falling step where its own first clause needs a climb. Checked over every list of length 1 to 8
drawn from `{0, 1, 2, 3}`, all 87,380: no list ever had two qualifying indices.

So the index phase one stops on is the definition's `i` if the definition has one at all.
**Everything before that index strictly rises by construction — the loop tested exactly that and
nothing else got past it — which leaves only the two end conditions and the fall to check.** That
is the correctness argument, and it is the whole of it.

## Complexity, and what it actually costs

Phase one examines the adjacent pairs `0` through `i`; phase two begins at pair `i` and runs on
from there. Exactly one pair — the step out of the summit — is looked at twice, and no pair is
ever looked at three times, so the total is at most `n` neighbour comparisons. Counted rather than
argued: over all 349,524 lists of length 1 to 9 over four values, the count never once exceeded
`n`. At `n = 10^4`, on the five shapes the suite times, it is 10,000 comparisons on a true
mountain, 9,999 on a strictly rising list, 10,000 on a mountain whose last step is flat, 5,002 on
one with a plateau at the summit and 1 on a list that falls from its first element. **O(n) time,
O(1) extra space.** `tracemalloc` over the whole routine at `n = 10^4`, ten traced passes,
identical every time: **0 bytes** peak.

The cheap inputs are the ones that stop soonest, and the walk never goes back — that last shape is
settled by the first comparison it makes, while the other four have to be read to the end. On those
four a pass at the ceiling costs a few tenths of a millisecond: measured over six rounds it stayed
between 0.12 and 0.33 ms, and the band itself slid by a third from one round to the next on an idle
machine, which is why the figure to hold on to is the ratio and not the milliseconds. Against the
suite's growth guard — 1,250 elements to 10,000, eight times the input — eight times the input
bought about eight times the work on every one of the four: every reading over those six rounds
landed between 8.0x and 9.5x, where the guard's ceiling is 25x.

## The approaches not taken

**The summit by `max()`, then the two sides.** Two passes at C speed to find the only candidate,
then one pass over each side of it. The same complexity class and the same growth reading, 7.8x to
9.1x over those six rounds. What differs is which inputs it is quick on, and that ordering is stable
because it follows from a property of the input rather than from the clock: where the summit sits
strictly inside the list, the walk is the quicker of the two, measured at 0.55x to 0.80x of its cost
over four rounds on the three such shapes the suite times. On a list that only climbs the ordering
reverses and is not close — the maximum is the last element, so the fence rejects after two passes
at C speed and no Python-level pass over the list at all, and there the walk measured 1.9x to 2.2x
its cost. Peak traced memory is 40 bytes when the fence rejects and 440 when both `all()` passes
run: the generator objects, and nothing that grows with `n`. Its one risk is the tie that
`.index()` cannot see.

**Slice at the summit and check each half.** Accepted here and accepted by the judge: a pass at
the ceiling costs the same few tenths of a millisecond as everything else in this section, and
growth on a true mountain measured 8.0x to 8.4x, squarely inside the bands above. The entire
difference is memory — peak traced 120,456 bytes at `n = 10^4`, drifting by 32 bytes between passes
and by nothing else, against 0 for the walk. A list holds one pointer per element at eight bytes
each, so that figure is the two halves plus the neighbour pairs of one of them, and it grows with
`n`. Nothing in this suite measures space and the constraints do not force it, so **constant extra
space is the better habit here and not a requirement of the problem**; chapter 5 is where the space
bound becomes the subject.

**Both ends inward.** Climb from the left, climb from the right going backwards, accept if the two
stop on the same index and neither stopped where it started. Growth 7.8x to 9.4x over those six
rounds, a per-pass cost inside the same band as the walk on the four shapes that have to be read to
the end, and 0 bytes peak traced. On those four it is a matter of taste: it reads the list twice and
states the three end conditions as three separate tests.

The fifth shape is where the two part company, and the difference is the walk's early exit rather
than anything about growth. On a list that falls from its first element the walk is finished after
one comparison, while this one still climbs the whole list from the right before it can answer:
measured at `n = 10^4` over four rounds, 0.05 us against 240 to 243 us, a factor of around 5,200.
Both are O(n), both are far inside both guards, and what separates them is only ever which inputs
they are allowed to leave early.

**The literal reading — every candidate summit verified from scratch.** It is correct, and it is
the reference the suite grades short inputs against, living in `tests/_reference/`. It is also the
one shape the constraints reject. At `n = 10^4`, over ten runs, it measured 301 to 312 ms a pass on
a true mountain and 1,210 to 1,330 ms on a strictly rising list, against the walk measured beside it
in the same runs — a factor of between 1,075x and 1,250x on the first shape and between 4,900x and
5,420x on the second. Eight times the input cost it between 68x and 75x, where every linear shape
here costs about 8x. The absolute milliseconds drift between runs and the ordering never moves, and
it is the ordering the guards read: run against them directly, this shape spends the entire ceiling
budget — 1.0 s for 25 passes — inside the first four of them, and it never reaches the growth guard
at all, which declines to measure once a single pass at 1,250 elements costs more than 3 ms. This
one takes about 4.2 ms there.

## Before you call it done

Run `uv run pytest tests/ch04/test_p02_valid_mountain_array.py`, then check by hand: `[7]`,
`[2, 1]`, `[1, 2]`, `[5, 5]`, `[0, 1, 2, 3]`, `[3, 2, 1, 0]`, `[3, 5, 5]`, `[1, 1, 2, 1]`,
`[1, 2, 3, 3, 1]`, `[1, 2, 3, 2, 2]` and `[0, 3, 2, 1]`. Exactly one of those eleven is True.

Confirm you return a `bool` and not the index you stopped on — the suite compares types, and an
index that happens to be nonzero is not `True`. Confirm `arr` comes back exactly as it arrived:
this list is input, nobody reads it after you return, and so nothing you could write into it would
be an answer. And resist sorting anything. `sorted(arr)` costs a pass and tells you nothing the
`n - 1` steps did not already say, while `arr.sort()` destroys the only thing the question was
about.
