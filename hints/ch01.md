**One tier at a time.** Read a tier, close the file, go back to the code. If you read three tiers

## 485. Max Consecutive Ones

<details>
<summary><b>Tier 1 — nudge</b></summary>

You can solve this while looking at each element exactly once, remembering only two numbers.
What are those two numbers?

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Tier 2 — observation</b></summary>

A run of 1s ends in exactly two ways. One is "the next element is a 0." What is the other?

Almost every wrong answer on this problem handles the first and forgets the second. Check which
of the parametrized cases in the test file is failing — the names in the comments tell you.

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Tier 3 — approach</b></summary>

Track `current` — the length of the run you are inside *right now* — and `best`. Walk the array:
on a 1, `current` grows; on a 0, `current` resets to zero.

The whole problem reduces to one decision: **at what moment do you compare `current` to `best`?**
There is a choice of moment that makes the end-of-array case require no special handling at all.
Find that moment.

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Tier 4 — full strategy</b></summary>

Update `best` on every 1, not only when you hit a 0. Then the final run has already been counted
by the time the loop ends, and you need no fix-up after the loop. Solutions that only update on a
0 must repeat the comparison once more after the loop — correct, but strictly more code and the
place the bug usually is.

O(n) time, O(1) space, single pass.

When it's green, ask me about the `itertools.groupby` version. It's a genuine one-liner, it's
arguably the idiomatic Python, and it's *worse* here for a reason worth discussing.

</details>

## 1295. Find Numbers with Even Number of Digits

<details>
<summary><b>Tier 1 — nudge</b></summary>

The array part is trivial — it's a count over a filter. The real question is a single-number
question: *how many digits does x have?*

There are three genuinely different answers in Python. Try to name all three before writing any.

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Tier 2 — observation</b></summary>

The three families:

1. **Convert to text** and measure its length.
2. **Divide it down** — strip one digit at a time until nothing is left, counting as you go.
3. **Use the magnitude directly** — digit count is a function of the number's size, and there's a
   standard-library function that gets at size.

Write whichever came to you first. Get it green. Then implement the other two — comparing them is
what this problem is actually for.

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Tier 3 — approach</b></summary>

On family 1: `len(str(x))` is correct and clear. Notice what it costs — it allocates a string
object per element just to throw it away. At n ≤ 500 that is free. At n = 10^6 in a hot loop it is
not. Knowing *when* the clean version stops being the right version is the skill.

On family 2: a `while` loop with `x //= 10`. Watch the terminating condition, and ask yourself what
your loop returns for `x = 0`. (Zero is outside this problem's constraints, but "outside the
constraints" is a habit worth not forming.)

On family 3: `math.log10`. Careful — this one has a trap, and it triggers on exactly the inputs the
test file clusters around.

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Tier 4 — full strategy</b></summary>

The `log10` trap: floating point makes `log10(1000)` land a hair above or below 3.0 depending on
the value, so `int(log10(x)) + 1` is off by one on some exact powers of ten. It's the fastest of
the three and the only one that can be silently wrong. Do not use it in an interview without
saying that sentence out loud.

Now the one that matters. The constraints say `1 <= nums[i] <= 10^5`. That is a *tiny* domain — at
most six digits. Even-digit numbers are exactly:

    10 – 99          (2 digits)
    1000 – 9999      (4 digits)
    100000           (6 digits, and it's the only one)

So the digit count can be skipped entirely: three range checks, no division, no allocation, no
loop. O(1) per element with a very small constant.

This is the actual lesson. Constraints are not fine print — they are part of the problem
statement, and a tight bound is frequently an invitation to a different algorithm. Get in the
habit of reading them before you start.

</details>

## 977. Squares of a Sorted Array

<details>
<summary><b>Tier 1 — nudge</b></summary>

Write out `[-4, -1, 0, 3, 10]` and, underneath it, the squares in the *same positions*:
`[16, 1, 0, 9, 100]`.

Don't sort it. Just look at the shape.

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Tier 2 — observation</b></summary>

The shape is a valley: it falls, reaches a minimum, then rises. Never anything more complicated —
that's forced by the input being sorted.

Which means the **largest** square is always at one of the two ends. Never in the middle. And once
you take that one, the same is true of what's left.

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Tier 3 — approach</b></summary>

Two pointers, `left` at index 0 and `right` at the last index. Compare the two candidates, take
the larger, advance that pointer inward. Repeat until they cross.

That produces the answer in **descending** order, which is backwards from what you need to return.
Two ways out of that, and one of them is clearly better — think about which, and why the other one
costs you something.

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Tier 4 — full strategy</b></summary>

Preallocate the result — `result = [0] * len(nums)` — and fill it **back to front**, from index
`n - 1` down to `0`, as the two pointers move inward. Each step writes exactly one slot, so the
loop runs n times, one comparison each. O(n) time, O(n) output and no extra working space.

The alternative — append in descending order, then reverse — is also O(n), and it's the one to
avoid: `.append()` re-checks capacity every call, `.reverse()` is a second full pass, and
`[::-1]` allocates a whole second list. Preallocating states the size up front and writes each
slot once.

Compare `abs(nums[left])` against `abs(nums[right])`, or compare the squares directly. Both work.
One of them does less arithmetic per iteration.

There is also a "find where the sign flips, then merge the two halves like the merge step of
mergesort" solution. Same O(n), and it's the more general idea — but it needs a binary search or a
scan for the pivot and two pointers moving outward. Worth writing after the first version, because
the merge pattern comes back repeatedly in this card.

</details>
